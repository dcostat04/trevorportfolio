How I workAnd where I learned it

Five things I now believe about building products. Each one came from something I built, and a couple came from getting it wrong first.

  1. Change what happens after the habit, not the habit.

    Icestasy's customers order on WhatsApp and are happy there. Instead of a customer app, I rebuilt everything that happens after the message arrives. The decision

  2. Never let software guess when a wrong guess is expensive.

    "Belgian" can mean two different products. Order Desk asks the salesperson instead of picking one, because a slow order is an inconvenience and a wrong delivery costs a customer.

  3. Plan for the day the clever part breaks.

    The AI parser is the impressive part of Order Desk, so it is also the part I trusted least. If the AI service is down, a rule-based fallback keeps orders moving.

  4. Earn trust on a small surface first.

    At Barclays I started alerting with the three or four storage arrays that mattered most. The team had learned to ignore noisy alerts, and a small set that was always right won them back. The story

  5. Agree on the metric before launch, not after.

    I measured order entry time carefully, but tracked stock-outs and wastage only informally. Now the strongest part of the Icestasy story is the hardest to prove. I won't make that mistake twice.

AboutThe longer version

I trained as a computer engineer, spent two years keeping a bank's storage running, and then went to study finance. Along the way I noticed that the work I enjoyed most was never the code itself. It was working out what people actually needed, deciding what to build first, and getting a room of different teams to agree on it.

I like problems that look small from the outside: a WhatsApp message that takes fifteen minutes to retype, an alert nobody reads. They usually turn out to be where the real cost is. I'm looking for a product role in B2B commerce, supply chain or fintech, where engineering, operations and money meet.

Outside work I'm a Goan, so football is not optional. I like history and business strategy, and I'm always planning the next trip.
