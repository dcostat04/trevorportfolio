Case study 03 · Barclays, Pune

Storage alerts that people trust again

Two years in a team where problems surface either early or expensively. The team relied on manual checks, and on alerts so noisy that people had learned to ignore them.

Role
Graduate Analyst (BA3), then Software Engineer (BA4)
Timeline

Area
Storage infrastructure, SAN

The problemAlerts nobody read

An alert that people ignore is worse than no alert, because everyone assumes someone is watching.

The storage estate generated plenty of signals, but the team had stopped trusting them. Real problems were often caught by manual checks. My job was technical, but the problem underneath it was about trust.

What I didIn the order I did it

  1. Started small, on purposeI built alerting for the three or four highest-impact storage arrays first, rather than the whole estate. A small set of alerts that were always right did more to win back trust than full coverage that was sometimes wrong.
  2. Added observabilityThen I added observability with automated anomaly detection, so the team saw problems forming instead of finding them after they hit.
  3. Automated the repetitive fixesAs a BA4, I automated more than 60% of remediation workflows. Earlier, as a BA3, I had automated switch-port remediation for Brocade and Cisco.
  4. Removed the weekly grindI automated production storage data handling that had taken 30 hours of manual work every week, and data accuracy improved by 38%.
  5. Fixed causes, not symptomsI ran root-cause analysis on 150+ operational issues, with 90% resolution accuracy.

LeadingBeyond my own work

I led a team of four through four to five SAN storage data centre recovery exercises, each delivered ahead of schedule, and trained new joiners along the way. I also co-developed the Barclays CE Idea Hub portal with a team of seven, which drew 300+ idea submissions across GTIS India. Day to day, the work ran on ITSM and Agile delivery.

Looking back

This is where I learned the habit I now use in product work: earn trust on a small surface, then expand.

It's the same reason Order Desk asks a salesperson about "Belgian" instead of guessing. A system people rely on has to be right before it is clever.
