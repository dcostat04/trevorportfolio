Case study 01 · Icestasy

Replacing a WhatsApp-run business without asking customers to change

Three products I pitched, built, rolled out and handed over on my own: an AI order desk, production planning linked to sales, and an HRMS. The most important decision was the product I chose not to build.

The businessAnd the problem

Icestasy makes ice cream and sells it to cafés, hotels, retailers and distributors across six regions. When I joined, almost every order arrived as a WhatsApp message.

A salesperson then retyped each message by hand. That took 10–15 minutes per order, and today the business handles 70–100 orders a day. The kitchen and factory found out what to make when someone told them, and stock planning was largely estimation.

None of these were dramatic failures. They were small costs repeated a hundred times a day, which is usually where the real money is.

The decisionWhat I chose not to build

The obvious answer was a customer ordering app. I argued against it.

Our customers were comfortable on WhatsApp. An app would have asked a café owner to download something new and learn it, all to make our work easier. Every customer who didn't switch would have been an order at risk. So I proposed to the owner that we leave WhatsApp alone and rebuild everything that happens after the message arrives.

Customer ordering appDownload and learn a new appLost orders while customers resist the switchRejected
Keep WhatsApp, rebuild the back officeNothingMessages are messy, so the parser has to cope with real-world textChosen

That moved the hard problem from the customer to me, which is where it belonged.

DecisionsThat I'd defend

It never guesses

When a message says "Belgian", it could mean Belgian Speculoos or Belgian Chocolate. A parser that picks the likelier one would be right most of the time, and the times it was wrong would end with the wrong product arriving at a café. Order Desk shows the salesperson both options and lets them choose. A slow order is an inconvenience. A wrong delivery costs a customer.

It keeps working when the AI doesn't

The AI parser is the impressive part of the product, so it is also the part I trusted least. If the AI service goes down, a rule-based fallback takes over. It is less flexible, but orders keep moving, and a sales team that depends on the system can't be told to wait.

Pricing and GST are generated, not typed

Each order is priced from the correct list for its region (there are six) and produces a GST invoice automatically. Nobody has to look up a price list or build an invoice by hand.

Beyond ordersProducts two and three

Production and inventory, linked to sales

Once orders were structured data, the kitchen no longer had to wait to be told what to make. I linked sales and production data into an operations app with live stock dashboards for the kitchen and factory teams. Every week the platform looks back over six weeks of orders, works out what each item needs, and flags anything with less than about two and a half weeks of cover. Stock-outs and wastage have fallen, though I tracked that informally. More on that below.

HRMS

Before building anything, I defined the attendance and leave policies with management, because software can't enforce a rule nobody has agreed on. The HRMS then covered attendance, leave, regularization and separation. It is live for 18 employees, and the rest of the team is being onboarded as it scales to 40+.

Rollout and handover

I scoped the first version of each tool deliberately small, drove adoption across sales, operations and HR, and refined the tools through audits and user feedback. I ran stock reconciliation to check that the numbers in the system matched the stock on the shelves. When my internship ended, I left product documentation and handover guides so the tools wouldn't depend on me.

I led all three end to end on my own: business requirements, process design, build, testing, rollout and stakeholder onboarding, using AI-assisted engineering to move faster than a small company normally could.

Looking backWhat I'd change

I would agree on success metrics with the owner before launch.

I measured order entry time carefully because it was easy to time. Stock-outs and wastage were harder, so I tracked them informally. They have fallen, but the most valuable part of the system is now the part I can least prove. Next time, the baseline gets measured before the first line of code.
